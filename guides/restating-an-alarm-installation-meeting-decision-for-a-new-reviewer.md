# Writing an Alarm Project Decision Summary for a Reviewer Who Missed the Meeting

A practical worksheet for a Sarawak coordinator to transfer an alarm project meeting decision, its approval date and one open administrative question to a reviewer who was absent, with a worked example on user-facing notice wording.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why a decision summary is not the minutes

When a reviewer misses a meeting about a burglar alarm installation, the minutes alone rarely answer the question they actually have: what was decided, when, and what is still open. Minutes record discussion. A decision summary records the outcome and the single administrative question that still needs an answer. Keep the two documents separate. The summary should fit on one page and should be written for someone who was not in the room. It should not repeat the debate, and it should not describe sensor locations, layouts or technical steps. Those belong in the installation record, not in a decision transfer note. The reviewer needs to know what they are being asked to accept, not how the equipment works. A useful test is whether the reviewer can read the summary in two minutes and state the decision back to you without asking a follow-up question about what was agreed. If they cannot, the summary is carrying too much meeting texture and not enough decision. Write it the same day, while the approval date is still easy to confirm from the meeting record.

## The five fields every transfer note needs

A concise worksheet can use five fields. First, the decision in one sentence, written as an action that was approved, not as a topic that was discussed. Second, the approval date, taken from the meeting record rather than from memory. Third, the decision owner, meaning the person who confirmed the decision in the meeting. Fourth, the open administrative question, stated as a question with a named person expected to answer it. Fifth, the proposed wording status, which records whether any user-facing text was approved or is still a draft. These five fields are enough to transfer a decision without turning the note into a second set of minutes. Leave out anything that is not needed to act. If a field is unknown, write unknown rather than guessing. A reviewer who receives a note with one honest gap can follow up. A reviewer who receives a note with a confident but wrong approval date has to reconstruct the meeting from scratch. The worksheet is a transfer tool, not a summary of everything that was said.

## Worked example: who confirms the wording of a user-facing notice

Suppose a fictional Sarawak coordinator is preparing a short notice for staff about an upcoming burglar alarm installation. The meeting agreed that a notice would be issued, but it did not agree who would confirm the final wording. The decision summary should record the decision as approved in principle, the approval date from the meeting record, and the open question as a single line: who confirms the wording of the user-facing notice before it is issued. The reviewer who missed the meeting can then see that the notice itself is not yet approved, only the intention to issue one. This distinction matters because a reviewer who assumes the wording is settled may approve a draft that has never been checked by the person responsible for staff communication. The summary should name the role expected to confirm the wording, not the individual, unless the meeting record names the individual. If the meeting record is silent, the open question stays open. Do not fill the gap with an assumption about who usually handles communications. The point of the transfer note is to preserve the gap so the new reviewer can close it deliberately.

## Proposed versus approved: a simple labelling rule

The most common confusion in a transferred decision is between text that was proposed and text that was approved. Use a labelling rule in the summary. Any wording that appears in the note should carry one of two labels: proposed or approved. Proposed means the meeting discussed it but did not confirm it. Approved means the meeting record shows it was confirmed, with the approval date. Do not use intermediate labels such as agreed in principle unless the meeting record uses that phrase and you can quote the date. A reviewer reading the summary should be able to tell at a glance which parts of the note they can rely on and which parts still need a decision. This rule also protects the coordinator. If a later question arises about what was authorised, the summary shows exactly which wording had been approved and when. Keep the labels visible in the body of the note rather than in a separate legend. A short note with two clear labels is easier to review than a long note with a status column that the reader has to interpret.

## What to leave out of the summary

A decision summary for a reviewer who missed the meeting should not become a technical brief. Leave out sensor locations, device settings, cable routes, zone names and alarm operation details. Those belong in the installation documentation and should be shared separately if the reviewer needs them. Leave out customer names and personal contact details. Leave out any description of how the system will behave when armed or disarmed. The summary is a record of a meeting decision and one open administrative question. It is not a handover of the installation itself. This boundary keeps the note short and keeps the reviewer focused on the decision they are being asked to accept. If the reviewer needs technical context to understand the decision, add one sentence of context and no more. A useful discipline is to ask whether each sentence would change what the reviewer does next. If it would not, it probably belongs in the meeting minutes rather than the transfer note.

## Where the brand context fits, and where it does not

A decision summary is an internal administrative document, so brand context should appear only where it helps the reviewer understand what was decided. If the meeting concerned a burglar alarm installation, the summary can note that the decision relates to that work without describing the equipment. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs CCTV systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and the same project context can appear in a decision note as a one-line reference to the site and the work package. Keep that reference factual and brief. Do not use the summary to restate service capabilities or to introduce related topics such as camera placement, NVR setup or remote CCTV viewing unless the meeting decision actually concerned them. The brand name is useful as an identifier, not as a substitute for the decision record. If the reviewer needs to understand the wider security scope, point them to the project file rather than expanding the summary. The note should remain a record of what was decided, by whom and when, with the open question clearly marked.

## A short checklist before you send

Before sending the summary, run a short checklist. Confirm that the decision is written as an approved action with a date taken from the meeting record. Confirm that the open administrative question is written as a question with a named role expected to answer it. Confirm that every piece of user-facing wording is labelled proposed or approved. Confirm that no sensor locations, settings or customer names appear in the note. Confirm that the note fits on one page. This checklist is an author heuristic, not a formal standard, and it should be adapted to the meeting record you actually have. If the meeting record does not support a field, leave the field marked unknown rather than filling it from memory. A reviewer who missed the meeting will trust the note more if its gaps are visible. The goal is not a perfect document. The goal is a transfer that lets the reviewer act without reconstructing the meeting, and without mistaking a proposal for an approval.
