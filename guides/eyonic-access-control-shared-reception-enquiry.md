# An Access Control Enquiry When Two Businesses Share a Reception Desk

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on preparing an access-control consultation brief when two businesses share a reception desk, including a roles-only responsibility note and questions for an authorised installer.

## Why a shared reception needs a written enquiry before any hardware talk

When two businesses share a reception desk, the front door is not the only thing that needs a decision. The desk itself is a boundary: visitors arrive for one company or the other, staff from both sides pass through the same entrance, and the building manager may control the corridor outside. Before anyone discusses smart lock door access or an access-control panel, the enquiry should be written down so that each business knows which decisions it owns and which belong to the other party or the building manager. This guide is for a fictional shared-office reception in Sarawak, and it is deliberately roles-only. It does not describe actual permissions, staff lists, schedules, credentials, layouts, configuration, bypass methods or installation steps. The aim is to help you prepare a consultation brief that an authorised installer can respond to without guessing who approves what. A useful brief separates three things: the physical door and its condition, the people who need to use it, and the approval path for each decision. When those are clear, the conversation with an installer becomes a scoping discussion rather than a series of assumptions. ## A fictional roles-only responsibility note for the shared desk

The following note is an example, not a template to copy without adjustment. It uses roles only and contains no names, no schedules and no credentials. Its purpose is to show how two businesses can record who approves what before an access-control enquiry goes out. Example responsibility note: Business A reception lead owns the visitor greeting process for Business A visitors and confirms which Business A staff need to use the shared entrance. Business B reception lead owns the equivalent process for Business B. The shared reception coordinator, if one exists, owns the common-area visitor log and the handover between the two reception leads. The building manager owns the main entrance door, the corridor and any common-area door that is not inside either tenancy. Each business owner or office manager approves access for their own staff and visitors. The authorised installer is responsible for recommending whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate, based on the door condition, user roles and backup method. This note is deliberately short. It does not say who holds which credential, how many people are on each list, or when access should be granted. Those details belong in a separate controlled document that the businesses keep internally. The note only answers the question that stalls many shared-reception enquiries: who signs off on this decision? ## Which decisions belong to each business and which belong to the building manager

In a shared reception, the most common source of delay is not the technology. It is a disagreement about who owns the door. A practical way to sort this is to list each decision and assign it to one owner before the enquiry is sent. Decisions that usually belong to each business separately include: which of its own staff need access to the shared entrance, how its visitors are greeted and announced, who inside the business approves a change to its own access list, and what backup entry method its staff should use if the primary method fails. Decisions that usually belong to the building manager include: any change to the main entrance door or its frame, any cabling that runs through common corridors or risers, any door that serves more than one tenant, and any work that affects fire doors or common-area security. Decisions that need a joint agreement between the two businesses include: the shared visitor log, the reception handover process, and any common-area access rule that affects both sides. This is a roles-only split, not a legal opinion. The point is to give the installer a clear map so that the consultation does not become a mediation exercise. If a door is owned by the building manager, the enquiry should say so and name the building manager as the approval contact for that door. If a door is inside one tenancy, that business owns the decision. The shared reception desk itself is usually a process boundary rather than a physical access point, so it belongs in the joint agreement column.

## Questions to ask an authorised installer before any access-control decision

Once the responsibility note is written, the next step is a set of questions for an authorised installer. These questions are designed to surface the information the installer needs without asking for configuration details or bypass methods. Example questions: Can you review the door condition and tell us whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate for this shared entrance? What backup entry method would you recommend if the primary method fails, and who would be responsible for testing it? Which parts of the work would you need the building manager to approve before you can proceed? How would you separate the two businesses' user roles so that each business keeps control of its own approvals? What information do you need from us about visitor handling at the shared desk before you can recommend a setup? What is the handover process after installation, including user testing and a simple written record of who was trained? These questions are deliberately about process and responsibility, not about specific hardware settings. The installer's answers should be recorded in the brief so that both businesses and the building manager can see the same information.

## How the brief fits a Sarawak shared-office project

A shared reception in Sarawak may sit in a shoplot, an office tower or a converted building, and the building manager's role can vary widely. That is why the brief should describe the physical situation in plain terms rather than assume a standard layout. For example, the brief might note that the main entrance is a glass door in a common corridor, that the reception desk is shared, and that each business has an internal door behind the desk. It should not include a floor plan with access points marked, because that is layout information that belongs in a controlled discussion with the installer. That example is useful context for a shared reception because it shows the kind of practical office concerns an installer may ask about: how people move through the space, how visitors are handled, and how cabling is kept tidy. The brief does not need to copy that project. It only needs to show that the enquiry is about daily operation, not just about a lock. If the shared reception also needs CCTV security systems, that should be a separate section of the enquiry with its own questions, because camera placement and recording decisions follow a different approval path from door access decisions.

## A short checklist for sending the enquiry

Before the brief goes to an installer, run through a short checklist. This is an author heuristic, not a researched standard. First, confirm that the responsibility note names a role for every decision, including the building manager where common areas are involved. Second, check that the brief contains no staff lists, no schedules, no credentials and no layout drawings. Third, make sure the questions ask for recommendations and approval paths rather than specific configuration. Fourth, agree internally which business will be the main contact for the installer and how the other business will be kept informed. Fifth, decide how the installer's answers will be recorded and shared with the building manager. Sixth, if the shared reception also needs CCTV security systems, keep that as a separate topic with its own questions so that door access and camera decisions do not get mixed together. A brief that passes this checklist is ready to send. It gives the installer enough context to recommend whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate, and it gives both businesses and the building manager a shared record of who approved what. For a Sarawak shared office, that record is often more valuable than the hardware discussion itself.
