# Recording a Proposed Hotel Linen Pickup Window Before It Is Confirmed

A practical handover-sheet guide for Sarawak hotel coordinators who need to record a proposed hotel linen pickup window, keep proposed, acknowledged and confirmed wording separate, and hand off open questions when the receiving shift changes.

## Why a proposed pickup window needs its own record

A pickup window for hotel linen usually begins as a suggestion, not a decision. A coordinator writes something like "Tuesday morning would work for the new batch" in a message, and by the next shift that sentence has been repeated as if it were settled. The risk is not the suggestion itself. The risk is that three different people hold three different versions of it: the coordinator who proposed it, the colleague who read it quickly, and the receiving shift who was told about it secondhand.

This guide sets out a small, copyable handover sheet for an invented Sarawak hotel, which we will call the Riverside Hotel throughout. The sheet separates three states of the same sentence: proposed, acknowledged and confirmed. It also shows how to hand off a question when the person receiving the linen changes shift before anything is settled. Nothing here is a pickup schedule, a service promise or a treatment instruction. It is a record-keeping method you can adapt to your own property.

One useful framing: a proposal is a question wearing the clothes of a plan. Until someone with authority answers it, the sheet should say so plainly. ## The three states: proposed, acknowledged, confirmed

The sheet works because it refuses to blur three different things.

Proposed means someone has suggested a window and written down who suggested it, when, and on what basis. It is not a commitment by anyone. Example wording: "Proposed by: night coordinator. Proposed window: Tuesday, early morning. Basis: new batch of linen is bagged and counted. Status: awaiting acknowledgement."

Acknowledged means the other side has seen the proposal and said so, without agreeing to it. Example wording: "Acknowledged by: receiving shift lead, 4.10pm. Reply: noted, will check with the team. No commitment given." Acknowledgement is valuable because it proves the message arrived. It is not confirmation.

Confirmed means a person with the authority to commit has accepted specific wording, and that wording is quoted in the sheet. Example wording: "Confirmed by: duty manager. Confirmed wording: 'Tuesday early morning window accepted for this batch only.' Scope: this batch only. Recorded by: coordinator, 5.02pm."

The discipline is simple: never upgrade a state without a quoted reply. If the reply says "should be fine", that is acknowledgement at most. If it says "yes, Tuesday early morning for this batch", that can be recorded as confirmed, with the sentence copied exactly. Keep the original message reference beside the quote so a later reader can check it.

A few notes on filling it in. Write windows in plain words rather than codes, so a relief staff member can read them. Quote replies exactly rather than paraphrasing, because paraphrase is where "noted" quietly becomes "agreed". Record the scope every time, since a window accepted for one batch is not automatically accepted for the next. And leave the open-questions box visible even when it is empty, so the next shift knows the question was considered.

## A sample exchange, filed line by line

Here is how an invented exchange at the Riverside Hotel might be filed. It is illustrative only.

Original message from the coordinator: "We have a new batch of hotel linen bagged and counted. Could we propose Tuesday early morning for the pickup window? Please confirm if that works."

Sheet entry, proposed: proposed by the coordinator; proposed window Tuesday early morning; basis is the new batch being bagged and counted; status awaiting acknowledgement.

Reply from the receiving shift lead: "Noted. I will check with the duty manager and come back to you."

Sheet entry, acknowledged: acknowledged by the receiving shift lead; exact reply quoted; what was not agreed: the window itself. Status remains acknowledged.

Later reply from the duty manager: "Tuesday early morning window accepted for this batch only."

Sheet entry, confirmed: confirmed by the duty manager; exact wording quoted; scope this batch only; message reference noted; recorded by the coordinator with time.

Notice what the middle step did. It prevented the first reply from being read as a yes. It also gave the coordinator a reason to wait rather than announce a window to housekeeping. If the duty manager had instead replied "probably fine, check on the day", the sheet would stay at acknowledged, and the open-questions box would carry the question forward.

## When the receiving shift changes mid-question

Shift changes are where proposed wording most often drifts. The person who acknowledged the proposal goes home, and the incoming shift inherits a sentence with no state attached. The handover sheet exists to prevent that.

Use the shift handoff box to state the current state in one word, then name the next action and its owner. Example: "State at handoff: acknowledged only. Next action: incoming shift lead to obtain duty manager's exact wording before any announcement to housekeeping. Owner: incoming shift lead." That single line stops the incoming shift from treating the proposal as settled.

If the question cannot be answered during the handoff, do not close the sheet. Carry it forward with the open question intact and note who is expected to answer it. If the answer arrives after the handoff, the incoming shift should add a new confirmed entry rather than editing the old one, so the history stays readable. A short line such as "confirmed after handoff by duty manager, wording quoted below" keeps the sequence honest.

It also helps to tell the other side that a handoff is happening. A brief message such as "our receiving shift changes at this time; the window is still proposed, not confirmed, and the incoming lead will follow up" keeps both sides aligned without pretending anything is settled.

## A short review routine before the sheet is closed

Before anyone marks a linen pickup window sheet as closed, run a short review. These are author heuristics, not established research, and the timings are examples only.

First, check the state word. Does the sheet say proposed, acknowledged or confirmed, and does the evidence match? A confirmed entry needs a quoted sentence and a named person with authority.

Second, check the scope. Does the confirmed wording say whether it covers this batch only or something broader? If the wording is silent, treat the scope as unresolved and note it.

Third, check the open questions. Is each one assigned to a person who can actually answer it? An open question with no owner is a question that will resurface on the next shift.

Fourth, check the handoff line. Could a relief staff member read the sheet cold and know exactly what to do next? If not, rewrite the next action in one sentence.

Fifth, check the references. Does the sheet point to the written confirmation, quotation or message it relies on, rather than paraphrasing it?

A five-minute review at the end of a shift is a reasonable example target, but the point is the checklist, not the clock. The sheet is doing its job when the next person can tell the difference between a suggestion and a decision without asking anyone.
