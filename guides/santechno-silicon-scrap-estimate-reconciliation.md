# A Silicon Scrap Recycling Enquiry When an Internal Quantity Is Still an Estimate

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on separating an internal provisional quantity from a provider-confirmed receipt or accepted commercial quantity in a silicon scrap recycling enquiry, with a fictional status register and a document checklist.

## Why an estimate and a confirmed quantity are different records

A silicon scrap recycling enquiry often begins before anyone knows exactly how much material is involved. A fab engineer notices that a batch is no longer usable, a warehouse coordinator flags a pallet that has been set aside, and a procurement team in Singapore is asked to open a conversation with a recycling partner. At that point the only number available may be an internal estimate: a figure typed into a spreadsheet by someone who has not weighed, counted or formally released the material.

That estimate is useful. It gives a supplier enough context to understand the shape of the enquiry and to say whether the conversation is worth having. But it is not the same thing as a provider-confirmed receipt, and it is not the same thing as an accepted commercial quantity. Treating the three as interchangeable is where administrative confusion starts.

A simple way to keep them apart is to give each one its own label and its own owner:

Internal provisional quantity: an estimate produced inside the sending organisation, owned by the team that holds the material, and explicitly marked as unconfirmed.
Provider-confirmed receipt: a quantity recorded by the receiving organisation when material arrives at its facility, owned by that organisation.
Accepted commercial quantity: the quantity both parties agree to treat as the basis for commercial discussion, owned jointly and recorded in writing.

The practical point for this page is narrow: an enquiry can be honest about an estimate without presenting the estimate as a confirmed figure. The rest of this guide shows how a fictional team does that.

## A fictional status register for a provisional enquiry

The register below is entirely invented. The amounts, dates, reference labels and role names are illustrative examples created for this guide. They do not describe any real batch, any real organisation or any real transaction, and they should not be read as a template for how a supplier will record anything.

The purpose of the example is to show how a team can keep an estimate visibly separate from a confirmation.

Row 1. Reference: ENQ-EXAMPLE-01. Material description: mixed non-usable silicon, internal label only. Internal provisional quantity: 40 units, marked estimate. Date raised: 3 March. Raised by: procurement coordinator (fictional role). Status: enquiry drafted, quantity unconfirmed. Source document: internal stock note, not a receipt.

Row 2. Reference: ENQ-EXAMPLE-01. Internal provisional quantity revised to 35 units after a second internal count. Date revised: 11 March. Revised by: warehouse coordinator (fictional role). Status: still an estimate. Note: revision recorded, original figure retained for audit trail.

Row 3. Reference: ENQ-EXAMPLE-01. Enquiry sent to supplier. Date sent: 14 March. Quantity stated in enquiry: 35 units, described in the covering note as an internal estimate. Status: awaiting supplier response. Source document: sent enquiry copy.

Row 4. Reference: ENQ-EXAMPLE-01. Supplier acknowledges enquiry. Date: 18 March. Quantity acknowledged: none confirmed. Status: conversation open. Note: acknowledgement is not a receipt.

Row 5. Reference: ENQ-EXAMPLE-01. Provider-confirmed receipt recorded. Date: left blank in this example. Quantity: left blank. Status: pending. Note: this field is completed only by the receiving organisation.

Row 6. Reference: ENQ-EXAMPLE-01. Accepted commercial quantity. Date: left blank. Quantity: left blank. Status: pending written agreement. Note: not to be filled from the internal estimate.

Three habits make this register useful. First, the estimate column never gets overwritten by a later figure without a note explaining the change. Second, the receipt and commercial columns are visibly owned by someone else, so no one is tempted to fill them in from the sending side. Third, the status column uses plain words such as estimate, acknowledged and pending rather than vague terms like done or sorted.

A team can adapt the shape of this register to its own systems. What matters is that a reader can tell, at a glance, which numbers came from inside the organisation and which came from outside it.

## What to put in the covering note

The covering note is where an enquiry either stays honest or quietly overstates itself. A short note is usually enough, provided it does three things: it says what is known, it says what is not known, and it says what the sender is asking for.

A workable structure for a fictional Singapore procurement team might look like this:

Opening line. State that the team wishes to open an enquiry about non-usable silicon material and that the quantity mentioned is an internal estimate pending confirmation.

Material context. Describe the material in the sender's own internal terms, and note that the description is not a specification. Avoid implying that the material has been assessed, graded or accepted by anyone.

Quantity statement. Give the estimate, label it as an estimate, and give the date it was produced. If the estimate has been revised, say so and give both figures.

Documents available. List the internal documents the team can share, such as a stock note or an internal transfer record, and be clear about which documents do not yet exist.

Questions for the supplier. Ask what the supplier would need in order to respond, and ask which confirmation the supplier issues and at what point.

Contact and ownership. Name the person who owns the enquiry internally and the role that will handle any follow-up.

One useful sentence to include, in the sender's own words, is a plain statement that the figure is provisional and that the sender understands a supplier cannot confirm a quantity before receipt. That single sentence prevents a later misunderstanding about what was agreed.

It also helps to avoid words that sound like commitments. An enquiry is not an order, an acknowledgement is not a receipt, and a discussion about a possible commercial basis is not an accepted commercial quantity. Keeping those distinctions in the note itself saves a follow-up email later.

## Which source documents to ask about

Procurement teams often ask which documents they should attach to a first enquiry. The more useful question is which documents each party is expected to hold, and at which stage. The list below is a set of prompts, not a requirement, and it does not describe any supplier's actual paperwork.

From the sending side, before the enquiry:

An internal record showing where the material is held and who is responsible for it.
An internal note explaining why the material is considered non-usable, in the sender's own terms.
A record of the estimate itself, including who produced it and when.
Any internal revision history, so a later reader can see how the figure changed.

From the sending side, at the point of sending:

A copy of the enquiry as sent, kept with the date.
A note of which documents were attached and which were withheld.

From the receiving side, at acknowledgement:

A written acknowledgement that the enquiry has been received.
A statement of what the receiving organisation will do next, without any quantity confirmation.

From the receiving side, at receipt:

A provider-confirmed receipt, issued by the organisation that received the material.

From both sides, at commercial agreement:

A written record of the accepted commercial quantity, dated and attributed to named roles.

What the checklist should not do is imply that the sending team can produce the receipt or the commercial confirmation. Those belong to the other side of the conversation.

## Questions to ask before the enquiry goes out

A short internal review before sending can catch most of the confusion described in this guide. The questions below are written for a fictional Singapore procurement team and can be adapted freely.

Is every quantity in the draft labelled as an estimate, with a date and an owner?
If the estimate has changed, does the draft show both figures and explain the change?
Does the draft avoid implying that the material has been assessed, graded or accepted?
Does the draft avoid implying that a supplier has already confirmed anything?
Is it clear which documents are attached and which do not yet exist?
Does the draft ask the supplier what it needs in order to respond?
Does the draft ask which confirmation the supplier issues and at what point?
Is there a named internal owner for follow-up, and a named role for receiving the response?
Would a reader who joins the thread later understand which numbers came from inside the organisation?

A team that can answer these questions is in a good position to send a clear enquiry. A team that cannot may find that its first reply from a supplier is a request for clarification rather than a substantive response.
