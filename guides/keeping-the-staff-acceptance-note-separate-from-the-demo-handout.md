# Filing a Staff Acceptance Note When a Smart Lock Demonstration Comes With Two Handouts

A public-safe filing guide for Sarawak premises teams: how to tell a general introduction handout apart from a blank staff review form, and how to keep the signed acceptance record internal, with a worked example and a version correction note.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why the two handouts cause filing confusion

A smart lock demonstration often ends with paper in several hands. Two items look similar at a glance: a general introduction handout that explains what the session covered, and a blank staff review form that invites comments before any decision is recorded. A third document, the signed acceptance note, is usually created later and kept internally. The confusion is rarely about the lock itself. It is about which sheet is a public-safe summary, which is a working form, and which is the record of a decision. Treat each as a separate document type with its own owner, date and storage location. The introduction handout can be shared with staff who attended. The blank review form is a working document until it is completed. The signed acceptance note belongs with internal records and should not be circulated as a general handout. Keeping those three roles distinct from the first day prevents a blank form being mistaken for an approval, or a handout being filed as if it were a signed record.

## Name each document before the demonstration begins

Give each sheet a clear title at the top before the session starts. Suggested titles, as an author heuristic rather than a standard, might be: Introduction to Smart Lock Door Access, Staff Review Form, and Staff Acceptance Note. Add a short line under each title stating its purpose in one sentence. The introduction sheet describes what was shown and who presented it. The review form asks staff to note questions or concerns and carries no approval wording. The acceptance note records a decision, the date and the names of those who signed. When the titles are fixed in advance, the person filing the papers later does not have to guess. This matters in an office where several people handle paperwork. A blank form with the word acceptance in its title invites exactly the wrong assumption. If a supplier's material arrives with generic headings, relabel your own copies before filing and note the change.

## Who owns which document

Assign an owner to each document type rather than to the project as a whole. A simple split works for most premises teams: the person who arranged the demonstration owns the introduction handout; the team lead who collects comments owns the blank review form; and the premises manager owns the signed acceptance note. Ownership means the named person is responsible for the current version, the filing location and any corrections. Write the owner's role, not a personal name, on the document itself so the record survives staff changes. Where a supplier is involved, the supplier's own review material can sit alongside yours as reference. Eyonic's published smart lock door access review notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use the choice between a standalone smart lock and an access-control panel is usually checked first. That is useful context for what your review form should ask about, but it does not replace your own signed record.

## Dates, versions and the correction note

Every sheet should carry a date and a version marker, even a simple one such as v1, v2. The date on the introduction handout is the date of the demonstration. The date on the review form is the date comments were collected. The date on the acceptance note is the date the decision was signed. These are three different dates and should not be collapsed into one. When a document is corrected after filing, do not overwrite the original silently. Add a short correction note at the foot of the new version stating what changed, the date of the change and who authorised it, and keep the earlier version. A worked example: a fictional Sarawak premises team files its introduction handout as v1 dated 3 March. A week later the team lead notices the handout lists the wrong meeting room. A v2 is issued dated 10 March with a note reading: corrected room name, no change to content, authorised by premises manager. The v1 stays in the file. Anyone reading the folder later can see the sequence without needing to ask.

## A worked record with generic roles

Here is a fictional example showing how the three documents sit together. The premises team of a small office arranges a smart lock demonstration. The introduction handout, owned by the coordinator, is dated 3 March and marked v1. The blank staff review form, owned by the team lead, is dated 5 March and collects comments from staff who attended. The signed acceptance note, owned by the premises manager, is dated 12 March and records the decision, the roles of those who signed and a reference to the review form. The note does not repeat the review comments; it points to them. In the filing folder, the three documents sit in date order with the correction note attached to the handout. Nothing in the folder describes how the lock is set up, who holds credentials or how the door is configured. Those matters belong elsewhere and are not part of a public-safe filing record. The record answers only four questions: what was shown, who reviewed it, what was decided and when.

## What stays out of the public-safe file

A filing guide is not a technical manual. Keep configuration details, settings, security layouts, device capabilities and credentials out of the introduction handout and the review form. Those belong in internal technical records held by the people who need them. The public-safe file should be readable by someone who was not at the demonstration and should not expose anything that would help an outsider understand how the premises is secured. This is a document-handling rule, not a comment on any product. It also keeps the file useful: a handout that drifts into technical detail becomes hard to correct and hard to share. If staff raise technical questions during the demonstration, note the question on the review form and route it to the right person rather than answering it in the handout.

## Where a supplier's material fits

Supplier handouts and reviews are reference material, not your acceptance record. File them separately and label them as supplier material with the date received. If your team is considering smart lock door access, the supplier's published guidance can help frame the questions on your review form, for example whether permissions and backup entry have been planned. ## A short filing checklist

Before the folder is closed, check the following, as an author heuristic rather than a standard: each document has a title stating its purpose; each has an owner recorded by role; each has its own date; the blank review form is clearly marked as a working document; the signed acceptance note is marked internal; any correction has a dated note and the earlier version is retained; supplier material is filed separately. If any item is missing, note it on the folder cover rather than guessing. A file that is honest about what is missing is more useful than one that looks complete. The aim is a record that a colleague can pick up months later and understand without a conversation.
